Abstract—The proliferation of digital disinformation presents severe challenges to democratic institutions, public health communication, and social stability. In 2020, Ahmad et al. [1] published an empirical study (Complexity, Article ID 8885861) asserting that ensemble machine learning methods combined with psycholinguistic feature extraction achieve superior classification accuracy over individual classifiers across diverse news domains. This paper presents an independent, end-to-end replication and empirical evaluation of that study. We evaluate ten machine learning models—four individual baseline classifiers (Logistic Regression, Linear Support Vector Machines, Multilayer Perceptron, and K-Nearest Neighbors) and six ensemble architectures (Random Forest, Bagging with Decision Trees, AdaBoost, XGBoost, and two hard-voting ensembles)—across four benchmark datasets comprising 68,580 articles (DS1: ISOT, DS2: Kaggle Fake News, DS3: Jruvika, and DS4: Combined master corpus). Because the commercial LIWC2015 dictionary utilized in the original study is proprietary and closed-source, our replication implements a transparent open-source LIWC-style approximation comprising 87 active linguistic measurements plus 6 constant-zero padding dimensions (yielding a 93-dimensional feature vector aligned structurally with LIWC2015's schema). Models were trained using a 70/30 stratified train-test split, scaled using MinMaxScaler normalization fitted across the 70% training partition (isolating the held-out test set while allowing internal validation folds to share scaling bounds during hyperparameter tuning), and optimized through 10-fold cross-validated grid search (880 total cross-validation fits across the four corpora). Our experimental findings broadly support the central conclusion of Ahmad et al. regarding tree-based ensemble efficacy: advanced tree ensembles (XGBoost, Random Forest, and Bagging) consistently achieved top-tier performance, with XGBoost achieving the highest cross-dataset mean accuracy (0.9327, compared to 0.9375 reported in the original paper) and Random Forest achieving top performance on the heterogeneous DS4 corpus (0.9126 accuracy). However, ensemble superiority was not universal across all ensemble architectures: AdaBoost (mean accuracy 0.8909) underperformed individual classifiers including Multilayer Perceptron (0.9122), Linear SVM (0.8985), and Logistic Regression (0.8916). Voting Ensemble 1 (RF + LR + KNN) achieved a mean accuracy of 0.9159, closely tracking the paper’s reported 0.9175. On DS2, where the original paper documented substantial performance drops among individual classifiers (0.28 to 0.37), our standardized preprocessing and scaling pipeline achieved stable base-learner accuracies between 0.8052 and 0.8685. This paper analyzes the implications of linguistic proxies, documents methodological safeguards against data leakage, and provides complete reproduction artifacts.
Keywords—Fake news detection, ensemble learning, natural language processing, linguistic inquiry, machine learning, replication study, XGBoost, Random Forest, cross-validation.

I. Introduction

The rapid decentralization of media consumption and the ubiquity of algorithmic social feeds have drastically reduced the frictional cost of publishing and propagating unverified information. Disinformation campaigns during major geopolitical elections, public health emergencies, and economic crises have underscored the urgent necessity of robust, automated computational mechanisms capable of verifying informational veracity [2], [3], [4].

Traditional content-based natural language processing approaches rely heavily on bag-of-words or high-dimensional n-gram term frequency-inverse document frequency (TF-IDF) representations [5]. While TF-IDF models often capture topical vocabulary within a single domain, they remain susceptible to domain shift; an algorithm trained on political commentary frequently degrades when deployed on scientific or entertainment journalism [6]. Furthermore, deceptive text is intentionally crafted to mimic the topical entities of legitimate news while exhibiting distinct stylistic, affective, and structural linguistic signatures [7], [8].

To address these limitations, Ahmad et al. (2020) [1] proposed a framework pairing psychological linguistic analysis with ensemble machine learning architectures [9], [10]. Utilizing Linguistic Inquiry and Word Count (LIWC2015) [11], the authors extracted 93 continuous stylistic features per article and evaluated a spectrum of individual and ensemble classifiers across three distinct datasets and a unified master corpus. Their findings asserted that tree-based ensemble methods (notably XGBoost, Random Forest, and Bagging) and majority voting ensembles achieve superior generalization and accuracy compared to standard linear and distance-based baselines.

Independent replication is a foundational requirement of empirical science. Computational studies frequently encounter reproducibility bottlenecks stemming from closed-source tools, ambiguous preprocessing procedures, undocumented data splits, or undetected data leakage. The objective of this study is to perform a rigorous, independent replication of Ahmad et al. (2020) [1].

Our investigation provides the following core contributions:

  • Open-Source Linguistic Implementation: Because commercial LIWC2015 software is proprietary and closed-source, we engineer an open-source linguistic approximation comprising 87 active linguistic measurements plus 6 constant-zero padding dimensions (yielding a 93-dimensional feature vector aligned structurally with LIWC2015's schema) using public lexicons and rule-based extractors.
  • Methodological Rigor & Test Set Isolation: We establish an audited pipeline enforcing strict held-out test set isolation, documenting that fitting MinMaxScaler across the full training partition permits internal validation folds to share scaling bounds during cross-validation, and applying 10-fold cross-validated grid search on training splits only.
  • Comprehensive Benchmark Evaluation: We evaluate all ten classifiers across all four datasets (DS1, DS2, DS3, and the combined DS4), recording Accuracy, Precision, Recall, F1-score, and wall-clock training times.
  • Objective Discrepancy Analysis: We contrast our experimental findings directly against Table 2 of Ahmad et al., identifying areas of close concordance (DS1, DS3, DS4, and Voting Ensembles) while formulating cautious hypotheses regarding the divergence observed on DS2 individual baselines.

II. Related Work

Automated deception detection in text has progressed through several methodological phases, spanning stylometry, semantic modeling, and deep representation learning.

A. Stylometric and Linguistic Analysis

Early deception detection research established that deceptive communicators exhibit measurable deviations in pronoun utilization, emotional intensity, cognitive complexity, and structural punctuation [7]. Rubin et al. [8] demonstrated that satirical and deceptive articles employ higher rates of informal punctuation, exaggerative adjectives, and affective phrasing compared to legitimate news. Pérez-Rosas et al. [12] benchmarked Linear Support Vector Machines (LinearSVC) over combinations of lexical and psycholinguistic features, reporting accuracies between 70% and 78%. Pennebaker et al. developed the Linguistic Inquiry and Word Count (LIWC) framework, mapping words into psychologically validated categories [11]. LIWC features capture behavioral cues without overfitting to domain-specific entities.

B. Machine Learning Classifiers

Prior benchmark studies relied on supervised learning algorithms. Conroy et al. [7] surveyed text classification methods, highlighting Logistic Regression (LR) and Support Vector Machines (SVM) as strong linear baselines for binary veracity classification. Logistic Regression provides calibrated posterior class probabilities [13], while Linear SVM constructs a maximum-margin separating hyperplane [14]. Non-linear baselines, such as Multilayer Perceptrons (MLP) and K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN), have also been explored, though KNN often incurs high computational latency during inference across large document corpora [6].

C. Ensemble Learning Methods

Ensemble learning combines multiple base estimators to reduce variance, reduce bias, or improve predictive accuracy [9]. Decision tree classifiers, formalized by Breiman et al. via Classification and Regression Trees (CART) [15], provide flexible non-linear representations but are known to suffer from high variance. To stabilize decision tree estimates, Breiman introduced Bagging (Bootstrap Aggregating) [16], which trains an ensemble of trees on bootstrap replicates of the training data and averages their predictions. Breiman subsequently introduced Random Forests (RF) [17], injecting randomized feature subspace selection at each candidate split to reduce inter-tree correlation and yield strong variance reduction [18]. Boosting algorithms, including AdaBoost [19], [20] and Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) by Chen and Guestrin [21], iteratively train weak decision trees to minimize empirical loss functions by upweighting previously misclassified instances. Voting ensembles combine heterogeneous models through hard or soft consensus voting [10], [22]. Ahmad et al. (2020) [1] demonstrated that these ensemble paradigms provide resilient boundaries against domain variation in fake news corpora.

III. Original Study and Replication Objective

The reference paper for this investigation is:

H. Ahmad, M. U. Asghar, M. Z. Asghar, A. Khan, and A. H. Mosavi, “Fake News Detection Using Machine Learning Ensemble Methods,” Complexity, vol. 2020, Article ID 8885861, 11 pages, 2020 [1].

The original study evaluated ten machine learning models across four dataset configurations using 93 features extracted with the commercial LIWC2015 application. The authors reported that ensemble models—specifically XGBoost, Random Forest, and Bagging—consistently outperformed individual learners, achieving overall accuracy rates up to 99% on DS1 and 90% on DS4.

Our independent replication aims to:

  1. Reconstruct the complete data preprocessing, feature extraction, scaling, and training pipelines from first principles.
  2. Substitute the proprietary LIWC2015 feature extractor with a transparent, fully reproducible open-source linguistic approximation comprising 87 active linguistic measurements and 6 constant-zero padding dimensions (yielding a 93-dimensional vector aligned with LIWC2015's schema).
  3. Train and tune all ten classifiers using 10-fold cross-validated grid search under strict train/test isolation.
  4. Evaluate whether the empirical superiority of ensemble methods over individual classifiers holds when evaluated using an open-source linguistic representation.
  5. Provide a rigorous, quantitative comparison against the published results in Table 2 of the original paper.

A. DS1: ISOT Fake News Dataset

The ISOT dataset [23] was compiled by the University of Victoria Information Security and Object Technology research group. It comprises legitimate news stories extracted from Reuters.com and fraudulent articles collected from untrusted domains flagged by fact-checking platforms such as PolitiFact. Raw totals comprise 21,417 truthful articles and 23,481 fake articles (44,898 raw total). Following HTML stripping, deduplication, and minimum word count filtering (≥ 20 words), DS1 contains 44,182 articles (22,766 Fake, 21,416 Truthful).

B. DS2: Kaggle Fake News Dataset

DS2 originates from the Kaggle Fake News competition [24]. This dataset contains full article text, headlines, author attributions, and reliability labels across a broad range of domains. The raw training split contains 20,800 records. Following cleaning and filtering, DS2 contains 20,408 articles (10,022 Fake, 10,386 Truthful). Note that while raw Kaggle records label unreliable articles as 1 and reliable articles as 0, our pipeline maps labels to standard research conventions (0 = Fake, 1 = Truthful).

C. DS3: Kaggle Fake News Detection (Jruvika)

DS3 is a smaller, multi-domain benchmark published on Kaggle by Jruvika [25]. The raw dataset contains 4,009 articles covering sports, technology, entertainment, and politics. Following text cleaning and length filtering, DS3 contains 3,990 articles (2,122 Fake, 1,868 Truthful).

D. DS4: Combined Master Corpus

To evaluate model generalization across heterogeneous journalistic domains, Ahmad et al. concatenated the three primary datasets into a master dataset. Our pipeline concatenates cleaned DS1, DS2, and DS3 records, applies a global random shuffle with a fixed seed (random_state=42), and resets indices. DS4 contains 68,580 articles (34,910 Fake, 33,670 Truthful).

A. Data Preprocessing

Text cleaning was standardized across all corpora:

  • Entity and Tag Decoding: HTML entities (e.g., &amp;, &quot;) were converted to standard ASCII characters using html.unescape. HTML markup tags (<p>, <br>, <div>) were stripped via regular expressions.
  • Metadata Filtering: Author attributions, publication dates, source tags, and URL strings were removed to prevent algorithms from memorizing non-textual identifiers.
  • Headline and Body Fusion: The headline/title was concatenated with the article body separated by a single space.
  • Length Pruning: Following Ahmad et al., articles with empty body text or fewer than 20 total words were excluded.

B. 93-Feature Open-Source LIWC-Style Approximation

Ahmad et al. [1] utilized the commercial LIWC2015 application [11] to generate 93 discrete and continuous variables. Because LIWC2015 is closed-source and its dictionary is copyrighted, our project engineered an open-source approximation in src/liwc_features.py. The extractor parses raw text and extracts 87 active linguistic measurements across 10 functional categories, supplemented by 6 constant-zero padding features to construct a 93-dimensional feature vector aligning structurally with LIWC2015's column schema:

  • Text Statistics (6 features): Word count (wc), words per sentence (wps), percentage of words longer than six letters (sixltr), dictionary word match proxy (dic), numeral count (numerals), and average word length (avg_word_len).
  • Punctuation Counts (6 features): Frequencies of periods (period), commas (comma), colons (colon), exclamation marks (excl), question marks (qmark), and dashes (dash).
  • Function Words & Pronouns (11 features): Total pronouns (pronoun), personal pronouns (ppron), first-person singular (i), first-person plural (we), second-person (you), third-person singular (shehe), third-person plural (they), impersonal pronouns (ipron), articles (article), prepositions (prep), and auxiliary verbs (auxverb).
  • Social and Affective Processes (11 features): Social words (social, family, friend, female, male), general affect (affect), positive emotion (posemo), negative emotion (negemo), anxiety (anx), anger, and sadness (sad).
  • Cognitive Processes (5 features): General cognitive processes (cogproc), insight (insight), causation (cause), discrepancies (discrep), and tentative language (tentat).
  • Perceptual and Biological Terms (10 features): Perceptual verbs (percept, see, hear, feel), biological terms (bio, body, health, sexual, ingest), and basic psychological drives (drives).
  • Time and Space Orientation (7 features): Past tense markers (focuspast), present tense markers (focuspresent), future tense markers (focusfuture), spatial/motion relativity (relativ, motion, space), and general time references (time).
  • Personal Concerns (7 features): Work (work), achievement (achieve), leisure (leisure), home (home), financial terms (money), religion (relig), and death (death).
  • Grammatical Markers (8 features): Verbs (verb), adjectives (adj), comparatives (compare), interrogatives (interrog), number words (number_wrd), quantifiers (quant), conjunctions (conjunctions), and negations (negate).
  • Informal Language & Readability (16 features): Informal speech (informal, swear, netspeak, assent, nonflu, filler), readability and lexical diversity metrics (unique_words_ratio, sentence_count, avg_sentence_len, stopword_ratio), sentiment polarity lexicon scores (sentiment_pos, sentiment_neg, sentiment_compound), URL presence flag (has_url), capitalization ratio (allcaps_ratio), and title-case ratio (title_case_ratio).
  • Dimensionality Alignment Padding (6 features): Fixed constant-zero alignment features (pad_88 through pad_93) explicitly defined in src/liwc_features.py to maintain exact 93-feature dimensionality matching the 93-column specification of LIWC2015.
  • Deterministic Order & Dimensionality: The extractor outputs exactly 93 features (87 active linguistic measurements + 6 constant-zero padding features = 93) with deterministic indexing across all records.

C. Feature Scaling and Train-Test Split

Each dataset was partitioned into 70% training and 30% testing subsets using stratified sampling with random_state=42. Stratification guarantees that the class ratio of fake to truthful articles remains identical in both partitions.

To normalize continuous counts and percentage ratios into a uniform [0, 1] interval, scikit-learn's MinMaxScaler was employed:

xscaled = (x − xmin,train) / (xmax,train − xmin,train)

Held-Out Test Set Isolation and Scaling Protocol: Scikit-learn's MinMaxScaler was fitted across the complete 70% training partition Xtrain. The held-out test partition Xtest was transformed strictly using the fitted training extrema without re-estimating parameters, ensuring full isolation of the evaluation split. However, because scaling precedes cross-validation, the scaling bounds reflect the entire training split rather than being re-estimated inside each cross-validation fold, as discussed in Section X-B.

D. Machine Learning Classifiers

We implemented the four individual benchmark algorithms specified in Section 2.2 of Ahmad et al.:

  • Logistic Regression (LR): Models class posterior probability via the logistic sigmoid function:
    P(y=1 | x) = 1 / (1 + e−(wTx + b))
    Optimized via the L-BFGS solver with ℓ2 regularization penalty controlled by hyperparameter C.
  • Linear Support Vector Machine (LinearSVC): Constructs a maximum-margin hyperplane minimizing hinge loss with ℓ2 regularization penalty:
    minw, b ½ ||w||2 + C ∑ max(0, 1 − yi(wTxi + b))
    We use the LinearSVC implementation with dual="auto" and max_iter=2000.
  • Multilayer Perceptron (MLP): A feedforward artificial neural network consisting of an input layer (93 units), one hidden layer with ReLU activation (h = max(0, W1x + b1)), and a logistic output unit trained via the Adam optimizer with early stopping.
  • K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN): An instance-based non-parametric classifier assigning the label based on the majority class among the k closest training points in feature space according to a distance metric d(x, z).

E. Ensemble Methods

We implemented the six ensemble classifiers detailed in Section 2.3 of the original paper:

  • Random Forest (RF): An ensemble of 100 decorrelated decision trees constructed using bootstrap samples of the training set. At each split, a random subset of features is evaluated using the Gini impurity criterion:
    Gini(t) = 1 − ∑ p(k|t)2
  • Bagging Classifier (Bootstrap Aggregating): An ensemble of 100 unpruned Decision Trees trained on random sub-samples (sampling ratio 0.8) drawn with replacement from Xtrain. Predictions represent unweighted average votes across all base estimators.
  • AdaBoost (Adaptive Boosting): A sequential boosting algorithm fitting shallow decision tree stumps (depth 1). At each iteration m, misclassified instances receive increased sample weights:
    wi(m+1) = wi(m) exp(αm 𝕀(yi ≠ hm(xi)))
  • XGBoost (Extreme Gradient Boosting): A scalable tree boosting system optimizing an objective function comprising a convex loss function and tree complexity regularizer Ω(fm) = γT + ½λ∑wj2. XGBoost utilizes second-order Taylor expansions to optimize split finding.
  • Voting Classifier 1 (VC1 - RF + LR + KNN): A consensus ensemble combining the tuned predictions of Random Forest, Logistic Regression, and KNN via hard majority voting:
    ŷVC1 = 𝕀(ŷRF + ŷLR + ŷKNN ≥ 2)
  • Voting Classifier 2 (VC2 - LR + LSVM + DT): A consensus ensemble combining the predictions of Logistic Regression, Linear SVM, and a CART Decision Tree via hard majority voting:
    ŷVC2 = 𝕀(ŷLR + ŷLSVM + ŷDT ≥ 2)

Hyperparameters were tuned via scikit-learn's GridSearchCV using 10-fold cross-validation (k=10) fitted exclusively on Xtrain. Scoring was guided by cross-validated accuracy.

VI. Experimental Setup

The experimental environment was executed under Python 3.14.3 on a 64-bit Windows workstation equipped with an Intel multi-core processor (12 logical cores) and 16 GB physical RAM. Dependencies included scikit-learn (v1.9.1), xgboost (v3.4.1), pandas (v3.0.6), numpy (v2.5.3), and joblib (v1.6.0).

To prevent memory exhaustion during 10-fold cross-validation on large datasets (DS1: 30,927 train rows; DS4: 48,006 train rows), cross-validation worker concurrency was controlled using GRID_SEARCH_N_JOBS = 2 with pre_dispatch = "n_jobs". Inner estimators were constrained to single-threaded execution (n_jobs=1), eliminating nested process proliferation. Garbage collection (gc.collect()) was enforced after every model evaluation.

A. Performance Metrics

Model performance was evaluated on the isolated 30% test sets using standard binary classification metrics:

Accuracy = (TP + TN) / (TP + TN + FP + FN)
Precision = TP / (TP + FP),   Recall = TP / (TP + FN)
F1-Score = 2 × (Precision × Recall) / (Precision + Recall)

where TP, TN, FP, and FN denote true positives, true negatives, false positives, and false negatives, respectively.

IX. Discussion

The empirical outcomes of our replication provide several insights regarding algorithm performance, linguistic representation, and cross-dataset generalization.

A. Generalization Across Heterogeneous Domains (DS4)

The master dataset (DS4) represents the primary stress test for cross-domain fake news detection, merging political, general entertainment, and sports reporting. On DS4, our experimental results match Table 2 of Ahmad et al. across almost every algorithm within an absolute margin of 0.01% to 1.8%. Multilayer Perceptron achieved 0.8999 (paper: 0.9000), AdaBoost achieved 0.8593 (paper: 0.8600), Linear SVM achieved 0.8616 (paper: 0.8600), Random Forest achieved 0.9126 (paper: 0.9100), and Bagging achieved 0.9066 (paper: 0.9000).

This close correspondence is consistent with the hypothesis that ensemble learning architectures provide strong inductive bias when learning decision boundaries over heterogeneous text collections.

B. Examination of the DS2 Base-Learner Discrepancy

As highlighted in TABLE VI, our results diverge substantially from Ahmad et al. on DS2 for individual base learners:

  • The original paper reported substantial accuracy drops on DS2 for Linear SVM (0.37), Multilayer Perceptron (0.35), KNN (0.28), and Random Forest (0.35).
  • In our audited pipeline, these same models achieved robust accuracies: Linear SVM (0.8546), MLP (0.8685), KNN (0.8052), and Random Forest (0.8805).
  • Discussion & Hypothesis: The exact technical cause of the low individual baseline figures reported in Ahmad et al. cannot be definitively established from the published text or available artifacts. It is notable, however, that raw Kaggle competition data uses an inverted label schema (1 = Unreliable, 0 = Reliable). If an uncalibrated classification threshold or partial label mismatch affected individual models in the original study, decision tree and voting ensembles might have remained resilient through consensus aggregation. This interpretation is presented strictly as a hypothesis. Importantly, on Voting Classifier 1 (paper: 0.8800 vs. ours: 0.8726) and Voting Classifier 2 (paper: 0.8600 vs. ours: 0.8520), our results align with the original paper within 0.8%.

C. Superiority of Gradient Tree Boosting and Precision Dynamics

Across all evaluations, XGBoost demonstrated the highest overall accuracy (mean 0.9327). XGBoost’s capacity to handle feature collinearity, apply ℓ1/ℓ2 leaf regularization, and prioritize informative structural features gives it an advantage over single-layer neural networks and linear baselines.

Regarding precision metrics, Random Forest achieved the highest cross-dataset mean precision (0.9384), followed by XGBoost (0.9333). In our experimental formulation, the positive class is designated as Truthful (y = 1), with Fake news designated as the negative class (y = 0). Consequently, positive-class precision measures TP / (TP + FP), representing the proportion of articles predicted as Truthful that are genuinely authentic. A high positive-class precision indicates high fidelity when verifying legitimate journalism—meaning that Random Forest rarely misclassifies fake articles as truthful (low rate of deceptive stories masquerading as verified content). Importantly, this positive-class precision does not directly establish minimal false-positive fake-news flagging (which would correspond to precision on class 0, or false alarms against truthful articles). Rather, it confirms that Random Forest's positive classifications carry low risk of fake-news contamination.

X. Limitations and Threats to Validity

A. Limitations

Several methodological limitations must be acknowledged:

  • Approximation vs. Proprietary LIWC2015: The original research by Ahmad et al. relied on commercial LIWC2015 software, which matches tokens against a proprietary, copyrighted dictionary of 6,400+ validated stems. Because LIWC2015 is closed-source, our replication implemented an open-source approximation utilizing curated word sets and rule-based heuristics. While our feature set captures the same conceptual categories across 87 extracted dimensions plus 6 constant-zero padding features, exact numerical values differ. Dimensional parity (93 columns) serves solely as a structural formatting interface and does not confer semantic or numerical equivalence with proprietary LIWC2015 dictionary stems. Our results should therefore be interpreted as validating the general utility of linguistic feature representations rather than an exact numeric reproduction of LIWC2015.
  • Absence of Sequential Semantics: Linguistic feature representations evaluate frequency statistics and lexical distribution but do not model sequential syntax, contextual semantics, or transformer-based attention representations. While effective for stylistic discrimination, linguistic proxies cannot evaluate factuality against dynamic external knowledge graphs.

B. Threats to Validity

We systematically identify potential threats to validity:

  • Construct Validity: Relying on open-source lexicon approximations rather than the proprietary LIWC2015 dictionary introduces potential lexical divergence, although the strong concordance on DS1, DS3, and DS4 supports the validity of the constructed proxy.
  • Internal Validity: Hyperparameter grids were bounded to discrete search spaces (22 combinations, 880 total CV fits) due to hardware constraints rather than exhaustive continuous tuning. Bagging was held to a fixed 100-estimator configuration. Furthermore, in our feature extraction and training pipeline, scikit-learn's MinMaxScaler was fitted across the complete 70% training partition Xtrain prior to initiating 10-fold cross-validated grid search. Methodologically, the held-out test partition Xtest remained strictly isolated throughout (the fitted scaler transformed Xtest without re-estimating parameters, preventing any held-out evaluation leakage). However, during internal cross-validation on Xtrain, each validation fold influenced the global min/max scaling parameters used across folds. While the held-out test partition remained strictly unobserved during model fitting and selection, this cross-validation design means that the validation fold within each of the 10 folds was normalized using extrema derived from all 10 folds. The precise empirical impact of this validation-fold leakage on hyperparameter selection was not quantitatively measured in this study because the experiment was not rerun with a fold-isolated Pipeline. Consequently, whether fold-specific scaling would alter the selected hyperparameters or downstream test metrics remains an open empirical question for future pipeline evaluations.
  • External Validity: While DS4 merges three distinct corpora, online disinformation evolves rapidly with changing rhetorical styles and multimodal formats (e.g., memes, video transcripts) that stylometric text analysis alone may not fully capture.
  • Conclusion Validity: A single fixed 70/30 stratified partition was evaluated per dataset, mirroring the design of Ahmad et al., rather than repeated nested cross-validation across multiple random splits.

XI. Reproducibility and Conclusion

A. Reproducibility Statement

To ensure scientific transparency, the complete experimental pipeline has been archived. The project preserves: (1) raw and cleaned data preprocessing scripts, (2) the open-source linguistic approximation extractor (generating 87 active linguistic measurements plus 6 constant-zero padding dimensions for 93-dimensional schema alignment), (3) model training and 10-fold cross-validation routines, (4) locked CSV evaluation logs, and (5) automated publication visualization scripts. All random seeds were fixed (seed=42) to ensure deterministic reproduction upon re-execution.

B. Conclusion

This study presented an independent, end-to-end replication and empirical evaluation of the fake news detection methodology proposed by Ahmad et al. (2020). By replacing the closed-source LIWC2015 software with a transparent open-source linguistic approximation (87 active linguistic measurements supplemented by 6 constant-zero padding features for 93-dimensional structural schema alignment), we audited the pipeline across four datasets totaling 68,580 articles.

Our experimental results broadly support the primary findings of the original study:

  1. Tree-Based Ensembles Lead Overall: Advanced tree-based ensembles (XGBoost, Random Forest, and Bagging) consistently achieved the highest classification accuracies across single-domain and mixed-domain corpora, led by XGBoost (cross-dataset mean accuracy 0.9327) and Random Forest (DS4 accuracy 0.9126). However, ensemble superiority was not universal across all ensemble architectures: AdaBoost (mean accuracy 0.8909) trailed individual classifiers such as Multilayer Perceptron (0.9122), Linear SVM (0.8985), and Logistic Regression (0.8916), demonstrating that shallow boosting stumps do not inherently exceed strong non-linear or regularized individual learners on stylistic linguistic representations.
  2. Master Corpus Generalization: On the combined DS4 master dataset, our replication aligned with published paper accuracies within 0.01% to 1.8% across nearly all models, supporting the premise that linguistic features provide viable signals across diverse journalistic topics.
  3. Utility of Open Linguistic Proxies: Competitive classification performance (exceeding 91%–96% on primary benchmarks) can be achieved using accessible, open-source linguistic approximations, providing an open framework for future research.
